Know It’s Absent, Yet Point Anyway: Cross-Task Semantic Contradictions
in Vision-Language Models
Anonymous ACL submission
Abstract
001
Vision-language models (VLMs) increasingly
002
support both visual question answering and spa-
003
tial grounding, yet their behavior across these
004
tasks can be inconsistent. In this paper, we
005
identify Existence–Localization Contradic-
006
tion, a failure mode where a VLM correctly
007
recognizes that a queried object is absent from
008
an image, but still produces a hallucinated local-
009
ization when asked to ground the same referent.
010
We introduce a conditional evaluation protocol
011
that pairs existence questions with downstream
012
localization prompts over the same image and
013
target expression, isolating cases where mod-
014
els know the referent is absent but fail to carry
015
this judgment into grounding. Experiments on
016
strong VLMs show that such contradictions are
017
common, revealing a gap between perception,
018
abstention, and localization. We further show
019
that lightweight interventions can substantially
020
reduce these failures. Our findings highlight
021
that reliable VLM grounding requires not only
022
accurate recognition, but also consistent refusal
023
to localize absent referents.

Introduction
025
Vision-language models (VLMs) (Liu et al., 2024;
026
Team et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025; Wang et al.,
027
2025; Bai et al., 2025b,a) can now follow natural-
028
language instructions over images and increasingly
029
support spatial outputs such as points and bound-
030
ing boxes. This makes them useful not only for
031
visual question answering, but also as language-
032
conditioned grounding modules in downstream sys-
033
tems. As a result, their reliability matters not only
034
at the answer level, but also at the level of spatial
035
commitments.
036
Prior work has shown that VLMs often hallu-
037
cinate entities when language presupposes their
038
existence.
Antidote (Wu et al., 2025) studies
039
counterfactual presupposition questions, JBA (Li
040
et al., 2025) evaluates false-premise recognition,
041
and Ground-V (Zong et al., 2025) considers hallu-
042
cinated references in complex grounding settings.
043
However, these settings do not directly test whether
044
a model that has already recognized object absence
045
can preserve that judgment when later asked to
046
localize the same referent.
047
We study this gap through a simple but important
048
failure case. A VLM may correctly answer “No”
049
to the question “Is there a cat in the image?”, yet
050
still return a bounding box when later asked “Point
051
to the cat.” We define this failure as Existence–
052
Localization Contradiction.
053
Unlike standard no-target grounding or false-
054
premise recognition errors, Existence–Localization
055
Contradiction captures whether a model preserves
056
an absence judgment under a downstream local-
057
ization request. The model semantically rejects
058
an entity, yet operationally commits to it through
059
localization output.
060
This semantic contradiction not only highlights a
061
violation of logical consistency between a model’s
062
semantic belief and its subsequent action commit-
063
ment, but also exposes a critical risk in multimodal
064
systems where localization serves as actionable
065
grounding evidence. A hallucinated bounding box
066
for a nonexistent referent can propagate false ev-
067
idence and trigger incorrect downstream actions.
068
We therefore characterize hallucinated localization
069
as a form of actionable hallucination. Such fail-
070
ures are particularly critical in robotics, medical lo-
071
calization, autonomous agents, retrieval pipelines,
072
and grounding-based tool use, where localization
073
outputs directly influence system behavior. Re-
074
liable grounding thus requires not only accurate
075
perception, but also consistent transfer of absence
076
information across task formats.
077
To measure this behavior, we propose a condi-
078
tional evaluation protocol. For each image and tar-
079
get expression, we first ask an existence question,
080
then evaluate localization only when the model has
081
correctly judged the target to be absent. This iso-
082

lates the contradiction of interest: the model knows
083
the referent is absent, yet still produces a non-null
084
spatial output.
085
We use this setting to answer two questions: (1)
086
how often do VLMs change from a correct absence
087
judgment to an incorrect non-null localization, (2)
088
how can these contradictions be reduced without
089
harming localization performance when the ref-
090
erent is present? To investigate the mechanism
091
behind this failure, we analyze model hidden states
092
under existence and localization prompts, and we
093
use these observations to motivate a lightweight
094
inference-time intervention. Our results suggest
095
that reliable VLM grounding requires not only rec-
096
ognizing when an object is absent, but also carrying
097
that judgment into downstream localization deci-
098
sions.

Related Work
100
Hallucination in VLMs.
Hallucination in vision-
101
language models (VLMs) has been widely studied
102
across several related settings. First, object-level
103
hallucination evaluates whether models incorrectly
104
affirm objects that are not present in the image.
105
POPE (Li et al., 2023b) shows that large VLMs
106
often hallucinate objects, especially when they are
107
plausible given the scene context or co-occurring
108
categories. More recent work studies a stronger
109
object-absence setting by explicitly removing ob-
110
jects from images and testing whether models still
111
“see” the removed entities (He et al., 2025). Sec-
112
ond, no-target grounding studies cases where a
113
referring expression has no valid visual referent.
114
RECANTFormer (Hemanthage et al., 2024) inves-
115
tigates referring expression comprehension under
116
varying numbers of valid referents, including no-
117
target cases , while GREx (Ding et al., 2026) gener-
118
alizes referring expression comprehension, segmen-
119
tation, and generation to single-target, multi-target,
120
and no-target settings. Similarly, (Yin et al., 2025)
121
propose the ROD dataset to help VLMs reject
122
non-existent objects under grounding queries ef-
123
fectively. Third, false-premise and counterfactual-
124
presupposition work examines whether multimodal
125
models can reject invalid assumptions expressed
126
in language.
Antidote (Wu et al., 2025) high-
127
lights VLM failures on counterfactual presuppo-
128
sition questions, and JBA (Li et al., 2025) further
129
studies false-premise recognition in multimodal
130
question answering. Together, these works show
131
that absent or invalid referents remain a persistent
132
challenge for VLMs. However, they mainly fo-
133
cus on existence recognition, premise rejection, or
134
whether the final grounding output is null. Our
135
work instead asks whether a model that has already
136
correctly denied an object’s existence will neverthe-
137
less produce a non-null localization for that same
138
object.
139
Consistency in VLMs.
Consistency has recently
140
emerged as an important dimension of VLM robust-
141
ness and trustworthiness. MM-R3 shows that mod-
142
els can achieve competitive accuracy while still ex-
143
hibiting substantial inconsistency under rephrasing,
144
restyling, and context perturbations (Chou et al.,
145
2025). Similar observations have been made in
146
broader work on multimodal consistency and ro-
147
bustness (Zhang et al., 2024). Our work shares
148
this motivation, but studies a narrower form of in-
149
consistency: a contradiction between two outputs
150
for the same image and the same queried referent,
151
namely an existence judgment and a localization
152
commitment.
153
Activation steering.
Many hallucination mitiga-
154
tion methods reduce unsupported outputs through
155
contrastive decoding, decoding-time penalties, or
156
post-hoc correction (Leng et al., 2023; Huang et al.,
157
2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2024). Or-
158
thogonal to these approaches, activation steering
159
methods intervene directly in hidden representa-
160
tion space without updating model parameters. A
161
common construction is the difference-in-means
162
estimator, where a steering direction is computed
163
from the mean activation of desired examples mi-
164
nus that of undesired examples. This idea appears
165
in Activation Addition and Contrastive Activation
166
Addition (Turner et al., 2024; Rimsky et al., 2024),
167
as well as in truthfulness and safety interventions
168
that identify linear directions associated with truth-
169
ful responses or refusal behavior (Li et al., 2023a;
170
Marks and Tegmark, 2024; Arditi et al., 2024). Re-
171
cent VLM work further suggests that hallucination-
172
related behaviors are encoded in intermediate hid-
173
den states and can be reduced through inference-
174
time intervention (Su et al., 2025). Building on
175
this line of work, we construct a DiffMean steering
176
vector from contrastive hallucinated and visually
177
grounded activations, and apply it at inference time
178
to encourage absence-consistent localization.

3.1
Problem Definition
181
We study a failure mode that we call Existence–
182
Localization Contradiction. Given an image v
183
and an object query p, we consider two queries:
184
(1) an existence query that asks whether object
185
o is present in the image, and (2) a localization
186
query that asks the model to point to, or otherwise
187
localize, object o in the same image. For example,
188
in Figure 1, the top prompt asks about the existence
189
of a person, while the bottom prompt asks for the
190
exact position of a person.
191
In this paper, we focus exclusively on object
192
absence, i.e., a setting in which the queried object
193
does not appear in the image at all. A trustwor-
194
thy model should satisfy two conditions simultane-
195

196
1. it should correctly deny the presence of the
197
queried object at the existence stage; and
198
2. it should abstain from localization, or return a
199
null localization, at the localization stage.
200
We say that a model exhibits an Existence–
201
Localization Contradiction if it (1) correctly pre-
202
dicts that the queried object is absent, yet (2) still
203
produces a non-null localization for that same ob-
204
ject. Figure 1 demonstrates the phenomenon: un-
205
der the object existence setting, the model correctly
206
recognizes that no person is present in the image.
207
However, in the object location setting, the model
208
still predicts the location of a person. This distinc-
209
tion is important. A model may fail because it does
210
not recognize object absence in the first place; that
211
is, an object recognition or perception failure. By
212
contrast, the contradiction we study arises when
213
the model already knows the object is absent but
214
still commits to a spatial prediction.

in Appendix A. In term of prompt settings, for ex-
453

provided by each benchmark. For localization, we

456

to reliably produce spatial grounding outputs. The

prompt can be found in Appendix B. We then evalu-
459

6
Conclusion
565
We introduced Existence–Localization Contra-
566
diction, a failure mode in which vision-language
567
models correctly recognize that a queried object
568
is absent from an image but still produce a non-
569
null localization for that nonexistent referent. To
570
study this gap between absence recognition and
571
grounded action, we proposed a conditional evalu-
572
ation protocol that pairs existence verification with
573
downstream localization under the same image–
574
query setting. Our experiments show that this con-
575
tradiction is common even among strong VLMs:
576
models may deny an object’s presence in text while
577
still assigning it a bounding box, sometimes with
578
high confidence. This suggests that localization
579
prompts can weaken or override prior absence judg-
580
ments, leading to actionable visual hallucinations.
581
At the same time, our intervention results show
582
that lightweight changes can substantially reduce
583
such contradictions while largely preserving lo-
584
calization performance on positive cases. More
585
broadly, our findings underscore the importance
586
of rigorously evaluating hallucinations in general,
587
as well as other model capabilities in particular,
588
rather than relying solely on question-answering
589
performance. Specifically, this need is most salient
590
in task-conditioned settings, where models must
591
translate their perceptual understanding into precise
592
and effective actions. Reliable visual grounding,
593
therefore, requires not only accurate recognition
594
and localization, but also consistency between what
595
a model knows about a scene and the actions it
596
takes. As VLMs are increasingly used in systems
597
where localization outputs guide decisions or phys-
598
ical interactions, preserving absence judgments is
599
essential for trustworthy multimodal deployment.
600
Limitations
601
This work focuses on a specific form of ground-
602
ing inconsistency, where a vision-language model
603
correctly recognizes that a queried object is ab-
604
sent but still produces a non-null localization. As
605
a result, our evaluation does not cover all types
606
of grounding errors, such as ambiguous references,
607
partial occlusion, attribute-level grounding, relation
608
grounding, or cases where the object is present but
609
visually difficult to distinguish. Our experiments
610
are also limited to a small set of open-source VLMs
611
and object-existence-oriented benchmarks, so the
612
findings may not fully generalize to proprietary
613
models, domain-specific systems, or more complex
614
real-world multimodal interactions. Finally, our
615
mitigation relies on a lightweight steering mecha-
616
nism that requires validation-based selection of the
617
intervention layer and coefficient; while effective
618
in our setting, it may depend on the model archi-
619
tecture and evaluation distribution, and stronger
620
steering could risk encouraging over-abstention in
621
unseen scenarios.
